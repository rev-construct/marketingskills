# Natural Transitions

Transitions tell readers how the next sentence or paragraph relates to the last one: more of the same, a turn, a result, or a step forward. Good signposting makes long content easier to follow and to skim.

The sections follow the transition types in the federal plain language guidelines ([plainlanguage.gov](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/organize/use-transition-words/), public domain). The phrases are written for this skill, in a marketing voice. Everyday connectives such as "however", "for example", and "so" are always fine in copy; the lists below give you fresher options so the same three words don't carry every paragraph.

# Transition types

## Sequencing

Use to preview what's coming and to move the reader from one part to the next. In long guides, a short preview orients readers; on marketing pages, start with the content instead.

**Previewing:**
- Here's what we'll cover...
- This guide walks you through...
- Below, you'll find...
- We'll start with X, then move to Y...
- The sections below explain...

**Moving on:**
- Now let's look at...
- Moving on to...
- With that covered, let's turn to...
- Now that you understand X, here's Y.
- That brings us to...
- Step two is where most teams stall.
- Once that's in place, the rest gets easier.

---

## Referring Back

Two light ways to connect a paragraph to the one before it:

- **Pointing words** point at something you just said: start with "this", "that", or "these" plus a noun. "This setup is what keeps costs flat."
- **Echo links** repeat a key idea from the last paragraph in new words, so the reader sees the thread. If the last paragraph explained a slow checkout, open the next with "That extra minute at checkout..."

Phrases that do the same job:
- As mentioned earlier,...
- As we covered above,...
- Remember when we discussed X?
- Building on that point,...
- Going back to X,...
- Earlier, we explained that...

## Adding a Point

- Plus,...
- On top of that,...
- What's more,...
- Another benefit is...
- Beyond that,...
- There's also...
- And it doesn't stop there.
- One more thing worth knowing:...

**Note:** Avoid "moreover" and "furthermore" in marketing copy. They read as AI-generated.

---

## Showing Similarity

- Likewise,...
- In the same way,...
- Just like X, Y also...
- This mirrors...
- The same applies to...
- It works the same way for...

## Giving an Example

- Here's an example:...
- Take X, for instance.
- Consider this:...
- A good example is...
- To illustrate,...
- Picture this:...
- Like when...
- Say you want to...

---

## Restating

Use when the idea is important or complex enough to say twice, in plainer words.

- Put simply,...
- Here's what that means in practice:...
- Translation:...
- The short version:...
- To say it another way,...

---

## Introducing a Result

- This means...
- That's why...
- Because of this,...
- This leads to...
- The payoff:...
- Which is how you end up with...

---

## Contrasting

- That said,...
- On the flip side,...
- In contrast,...
- Unlike X, Y...
- While X is true, Y...
- Despite this,...
- Here's the catch:...
- The trade-off is...

---

## Summing Up

**Summarising a section:**
- To recap,...
- Quick summary:...
- The takeaway:...

**Closing the piece:**
- Wrapping up,...
- The bottom line is...
- Here's what to do next:...
- Final thoughts:...
- Ready to get started?...
- Now it's your turn.

**Note:** Avoid "In conclusion" at the start of a paragraph. It's overused and signals AI writing.

## Providing Evidence

Use when citing sources, data, or expert opinions:

### Neutral attribution
- According to [Source],...
- [Source] reports that...
- Research shows that...
- Data from [Source] indicates...
- A study by [Source] found...

### Expert quotes
- As [Expert] puts it,...
- [Expert] explains,...
- In the words of [Expert],...
- [Expert] notes that...

### Supporting claims
- This is backed by...
- Evidence suggests...
- The numbers confirm...
- This aligns with findings from...

---

## Question-Based Transitions

Useful in long-form content for conversational tone and featured snippet optimization. Use them as headings or questions the reader would really ask, then answer with substance. Don't use them as a setup for your own punchline, and keep them out of landing-page copy (see [ai-tells.md](ai-tells.md)).

- So what does this mean for you?
- How do you actually do this?
- Wondering where to start?

---

## List Introductions

For numbered lists and step-by-step content:

- Here's how to do it:
- Follow these steps:
- The process is straightforward:
- Here's what you need to know:
- Key things to consider:
- The main factors are:

---

## Hedging Language

For claims that need qualification or aren't absolute:

- may, might, could
- tends to, generally
- often, usually, typically
- in most cases
- it appears that
- evidence suggests
- this can help

---

## Best Practice Guidelines

1. **Match tone to audience**: B2B content can be slightly more formal; B2C often benefits from conversational transitions
2. **Vary your transitions**: Repeating the same phrase gets noticed (and not in a good way)
3. **Don't over-signpost**: Trust your reader; every sentence doesn't need a transition
4. **Use for scannability**: Transitions at paragraph starts help skimmers navigate
5. **Keep it natural**: Read aloud; if it sounds forced, simplify
6. **Front-load key info**: Put the important word or phrase early in the transition

## Transitions to Avoid (AI Tells)

These phrases are overused in AI-generated content:

- "That being said,..."
- "It's worth noting that..."
- "At its core,..."
- "In today's digital landscape,..."
- "When it comes to the realm of..."
- "This begs the question..."
- "Let's delve into..."

See [ai-tells.md](ai-tells.md) for the full copy blacklist, including structural patterns like contrast reveals and negation lists.
